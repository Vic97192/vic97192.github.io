Black-Box Model Extraction under Distribution Shift

Motivation

Many useful models are available only through prediction interfaces. A user can submit an input and observe a response, but does not necessarily have access to the original training data, parameters, or gradients. This motivates a basic question: how much information about a model can be obtained through carefully selected queries?

In a conventional supervised-learning setting, labeled data are assumed to come from a relevant distribution. Black-box extraction is different. The available queries may originate from unrelated datasets, generative models, or simulations. Even when two query sets contain the same number of images, they may expose very different parts of the teacher's behavior.

My research examines this mismatch. I am interested not only in whether a student reproduces a teacher's predictions, but also in why certain query distributions transfer useful information and others do not.

Research questions

  • How does the support of an out-of-distribution query set affect the behavior a student can recover from a teacher?
  • Can response-space or representation-space geometry help diagnose insufficient query coverage?
  • When do differences in student pretraining or initialization limit extraction, even when query information is available?
  • Can we separate meaningful coverage expansion from changes that merely produce large prediction differences?

Approach

I use controlled teacher–student experiments with limited black-box queries. Depending on the benchmark, a teacher is trained for a target recognition task, while a student learns from the teacher's outputs on an available query distribution. I compare in-domain and out-of-domain sources, transfer settings, and extraction procedures such as Knockoff Nets and Data-Free Model Extraction (DFME).

I also study the geometry of responses and representations. Rather than summarize an entire experiment with a single accuracy number, I inspect which regions are visited by query data and how these regions relate to changes in teacher behavior. Some experiments have used tools from topological data analysis, including Mapper, to investigate structural differences between in-domain and out-of-domain representations.

Preliminary observations

The experiments motivate a distinction between having many queries and having useful query support. An out-of-domain source can provide numerous inputs without sufficiently probing the parts of a teacher that matter for a target task. Furthermore, student initialization and pretraining can impose additional limits: a query set may reveal information while the chosen student still fails to translate it into strong downstream performance.

These are research observations rather than universal claims. Their strength depends on the datasets, budgets, student architectures, and evaluation protocol. I am continuing to investigate how to measure and improve coverage under more realistic constraints.

Why it matters

Understanding query support helps clarify the boundary between access to a model's API and access to its learned functionality. It also provides a way to evaluate model-extraction risks more carefully: restrictions on training-data access may not fully prevent a model from being probed with public or synthetic inputs. At the same time, it helps explain why some seemingly rich query sets do not yield effective students.

Current status

Ongoing. I am refining the evaluation of query usefulness and connecting these observations to structured query-generation methods. No publication or accepted paper is claimed here.
